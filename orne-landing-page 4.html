Why we make fewer, better things

Most clothing is designed to be replaced. We design against that — choosing cloth for how it ages, not just how it photographs, and building each piece to be repaired rather than discarded.

That means smaller runs, longer lead times, and a workshop that knows the name of every garment it sends out. It's slower. We think it's worth it.

How a garment gets made

Every piece follows the same four stages, from raw cloth to finished coat — nothing skipped, nothing rushed to hit a season.

01

Sourcing

Cloth is chosen twice a year, in person, at the mill in Biella.

Our story

In 1998, Isabel Duarte set down her violin and picked up her grandfather's shears. He had cut cloth on the Rua do Alecrim for forty years; she had spent a decade touring concert halls. When his workroom came to her, she kept his cutting table, his pattern blocks, and one rule scrawled inside the door: nothing leaves this room unless it can be mended, not just replaced.

She named the house Orne, after the Latin ornare — to equip, to furnish, to adorn — a word that sits closer to armour than accessory. It seemed the right idea for clothes meant to be lived in for years, not one season. The tools on that table are the same ones still in use today, passed to the cutters she trained herself.

— Isabel Duarte, founder
